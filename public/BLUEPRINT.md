# Google Agentic Browser — Agents Edition: 12,000+ Word Project Blueprint

## Executive Summary

The Google Agentic Browser — Agents Edition is a browser shell that treats autonomous agents as first-class collaborators rather than as chat widgets attached to a tab strip. The product is defined by a single proposition: a user states an outcome in ordinary language, a team of specialised agents plans and executes the work across the open web and the user's Google Workspace, and every consequential action remains subject to explicit human approval, complete recording, and reversible state. The browser does not replace browsing. It reorganises browsing around missions, spaces, and verifiable agent identity.

The reference implementation described in this blueprint is a Manifest V3 Chrome extension that provides a full browser shell inside Chrome: Spaces for context separation, pinned applications, tab search, split views for one to four panes, Focus and Zen modes, a breathing ambient background, and a Home surface that unifies weather, calendar, tasks, Keep notes, Drive files, Translate, and Discover. Six agents carry distinct responsibilities. Nova Prime orchestrates. Scout Fox searches. Professor Octo ranks. Sentinel Owl verifies safety, policy, and spend. Bee Buyer pays through x402. Scribe Turtle records outcomes to Drive, Docs, Calendar, and email. Each agent carries a Passport that discloses its decentralised identifier, model, capabilities, spend limit, reputation, and audit history. No agent acts anonymously.

The reference mission that anchors the entire design is a thirteen-step procurement and research task: Scout searches, Octo ranks the pg-mcp-gateway candidate, Sentinel halts execution at a 2.00 USDC checkpoint, Bee pays only after approval, and Scribe persists the result to Drive, writes a Doc, creates a Calendar event, and emails a summary. The mission demonstrates the central architectural invariant of the system: planning is cheap and reversible, execution is gated, payment is explicit, and persistence is evidence. Twin Mode provides a dry-run simulation of the same mission with no funds moved. Recorder provides a scrubable flight log that rewinds any step. The human gates are not decorative. A denial stops the mission with no funds moved, and the system records the denial as part of the mission record.

The blueprint is written for a team that must ship a credible, reviewable, and legally defensible product. It therefore treats three constraints as non-negotiable. First, the product is not a Google product and must not imply endorsement, affiliation, or sponsorship by Google. Google names appear in plain text for informational reference, in accordance with Google brand guidance, and the product name, domain, and visual identity must remain distinct [[1]](https://about.google/brand-resource-center/guidance/?authuser=2&hl=es-419). Second, the extension must comply with Manifest V3, with its ephemeral service worker, offscreen-document escape hatch, declarative network rules, and prohibition on remotely hosted code [[2]](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/extensions/develop/migrate/checklist). Third, the agent system must respect granular OAuth consent, incremental authorization, and the principle that a user may grant Calendar without granting Gmail, and may revoke either at any time [[3]](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/resources/granular-permissions). These constraints shape every section that follows, from the naming decision to the token lifecycle.

The competitive context makes the timing material. Chrome retains approximately 64.7 percent of desktop share, yet new-install data in 2026 shows meaningful movement toward AI-native browsers, with ChatGPT Atlas and Perplexity Comet together capturing more than one third of new installs in a single quarter [[4]](https://www.readsignal.io/article/ai-browser-war-comet-dia-atlas-arc-2026). Google responded with Gemini in Chrome, a persistent side panel, Nano Banana image tools, and Auto Browse, an agent-style feature for multi-step tasks that requires confirmation before sensitive steps [[5]](https://vancouvermessenger.com/google-expands-gemini-chrome-task-automation/). The opportunity for an Agents Edition is not to duplicate Auto Browse. It is to provide the missing infrastructure that Auto Browse does not expose: multi-agent orchestration with verifiable identity, a legible mission ledger, explicit payment checkpoints, and a recorder that makes agent behaviour auditable after the fact. The product competes on accountability, not on model quality alone.

The economic model is equally explicit. Gemini grounding, Workspace API quotas, and x402 settlement each impose measurable costs. Grounding with Google Search on Gemini 3 and newer models is metered per search query, with a free allowance and a per-thousand price beyond it, and Gmail, Drive, and Calendar each impose per-user and per-project quotas that punish naive polling. The blueprint therefore specifies caching, batching, backoff, budget envelopes per mission, and a cost ledger that is visible to the user before payment. A mission that cannot state its expected token, grounding, and settlement cost is not ready to execute.

The remainder of this blueprint proceeds from product definition to protocol, platform, integration, safety, compliance, economics, engineering, roadmap, and risk. Each section closes with acceptance criteria that a reviewer can test without access to the original prototype. The document is intended to be sufficient for a senior engineer, a designer, a compliance reviewer, and a product lead to build the same system independently and to recognise when a proposed change violates the invariants.

## 1. Vision, Principles, and Non-Goals

### 1.1 Vision statement

The browser becomes a place where outcomes are requested, planned, verified, and recorded, rather than a place where tabs accumulate while the user performs the integration work by hand. The vision is deliberately narrow. The system does not claim to be a general superintelligence, a replacement for human judgement, or a new web. It claims to be a trustworthy delegation surface for a defined class of tasks: research across tabs and documents, comparison and ranking of candidates, drafting and filing of records, scheduling, communication, and small, capped payments for digital goods and API access. Each task is expressed as a mission with a visible plan, a visible cost, and a visible record.

### 1.2 Design principles

Seven principles govern every decision in this blueprint. They are stated here because later sections appeal to them when trade-offs arise.

**Accountability over autonomy.** An agent may propose, draft, rank, and summarise without approval. It may not send, spend, delete, share, or publish without approval when the action is consequential. The distinction is not a matter of model confidence. It is a matter of effect on the world. The system encodes the distinction in a capability matrix and enforces it in the service worker, not in a prompt.

**Identity before capability.** No agent executes without a Passport. The Passport binds a decentralised identifier to a human-readable name, a model identifier, a capability list, a spend limit, a reputation score, and a link to its audit history. The design follows the W3C Decentralised Identifier model, in which control is proven cryptographically without a central issuing authority, and applies it to non-human actors. An agent without a resolvable DID is treated as unauthenticated, regardless of how persuasive its output appears.

**Declarative interfaces, never executable code.** The agent never emits HTML or JavaScript for the client to execute. It emits A2UI declarative JSON that the client renders with its own trusted components. A2UI 1.0, released as the first stable version of Google's open protocol for generative UI on 11 November 2026, provides bidirectional function calls, accessibility support, and extensions, with renderers for React, Angular, Lit, Flutter, SwiftUI, and Jetpack Compose [[6]](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-10-build-delightful-fast-reliable-agentic-experiences/). The catalog is the security boundary. An agent cannot request a component that is not in the catalog. The client validates every message against the schema it owns.

**Local-first reactivity, server round-trips on action.** Following the A2UI binding model, typing into a field updates the local data model immediately and reactively, but state reaches the server only when the user fires an explicit action [[7]](https://mikezupper.com/posts/a2ui-agent-to-user-interface-protocol/). The design reduces latency, reduces cost, and preserves the semantic meaning of a click as consent. A draft is not a commitment. A click is.

**Least privilege, incrementally granted.** The extension requests only the scopes required for the task at hand, at the moment the task requires them. Granular permissions mean the user may grant Calendar without granting Gmail, and the system must degrade gracefully when a scope is withheld [[3]](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/resources/granular-permissions). The system checks granted scopes before every mission step and disables the corresponding capability with a clear explanation, rather than failing opaquely.

**Reversibility and evidence.** Every mission produces a record that can be inspected, replayed, and, where the underlying service permits, reversed. The Recorder stores the plan, the tool calls, the approvals, the payment receipts, and the persisted artefacts. The record is the product as much as the outcome. A user who cannot explain what the agent did has not been served, even if the outcome was correct.

**Calm by default.** The browser shell is ambient, not interruptive. Breathing backgrounds, Focus and Zen modes, and a Home surface that summarises rather than notifies reflect a deliberate stance: attention is the scarcest resource, and an agentic browser that demands constant attention has failed. Notifications are batched, gates are consolidated, and the system prefers a single approval card to a sequence of modal dialogs.

### 1.3 Non-goals

The blueprint explicitly excludes several ambitions that would dilute the product or create unmanageable liability. The system is not a new browser engine. It is a Manifest V3 extension that runs inside Chrome and respects Chrome's extension lifecycle. It is not a general-purpose operating system, a cryptocurrency wallet, or a marketplace for agents. It does not train models on user data, does not sell user data, and does not perform fully automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects without meaningful human review. It does not bypass paywalls, circumvent access controls, or automate credential entry on behalf of the user without explicit, per-site consent. It does not claim Google endorsement, and it does not use Google trademarks in its own name, domain, or slogan [[1]](https://about.google/brand-resource-center/guidance/?authuser=2&hl=es-419). These exclusions are not marketing hedges. They are scope decisions that keep the engineering, legal, and support burden tractable.

### 1.4 Success criteria

The product succeeds when a user can describe a multi-step outcome, inspect a plan that names each agent, each tool, each cost, and each gate, approve once at the payment checkpoint, and receive a complete, verifiable record without having to supervise every intermediate step. Quantitative success criteria appear in Section 13. Qualitative success is simpler: the user trusts the system enough to delegate again, and can explain what happened to a colleague without opening the code.

## 2. Product Definition, Personas, and Jobs

### 2.1 Product definition

The Agents Edition is a Chrome extension that provides a browser shell, a mission runner, an agent roster, and a recorder. It integrates with Google Workspace through OAuth, with the open web through tabs and search, and with paid APIs through x402. It renders agent output through A2UI surfaces, not through free-form chat alone. It persists outcomes to Drive, Docs, Calendar, and Gmail, and it exposes every action in a scrubable log.

The product is delivered as three interlocking surfaces. The Browser Shell is the always-present environment: Spaces, pinned apps, tab search, split views, Focus and Zen modes, and the Home surface. The Mission Runner is the transient execution surface: plan, steps, gates, receipts, and artefacts. The Agent Roster is the persistent trust surface: Passports, DIDs, spend limits, reputation, and audit history. A user may live entirely in the shell without running a mission. A mission may not run without the roster and the recorder.

### 2.2 Personas

**The researcher.** A knowledge worker who compares tools, vendors, papers, or datasets across many tabs and documents. The researcher needs cited synthesis, a ranking with explicit criteria, and a Doc that records the decision. The researcher values traceability over speed, and will reject a ranking that cannot show its evidence.

**The operator.** A founder, office manager, or community organiser who coordinates schedules, drafts communications, files records, and pays for small digital goods. The operator needs reliability, a single approval point, and a record that can be forwarded to a collaborator. The operator values the absence of surprises over the presence of novelty.

**The builder.** A developer or technical evaluator who tests MCP servers, APIs, and agent tools. The builder needs to inspect tool calls, verify signatures, reproduce a mission in Twin Mode, and export a log. The builder values determinism, explicit schemas, and the ability to rewind.

**The reviewer.** A compliance lead, auditor, or team lead who did not run the mission but must approve its consequences. The reviewer needs a Passport, a cost ledger, a gate record, and a list of artefacts with links. The reviewer values completeness and immutability over narrative polish.

### 2.3 Jobs to be done

The product serves six jobs that recur across personas. First, find and rank candidates against stated criteria, with evidence. Second, draft and file a record that a third party can verify. Third, coordinate time across people and documents. Fourth, communicate a decision with the evidence attached. Fifth, pay a small amount for a digital good or API call with explicit approval and a receipt. Sixth, prove afterwards what happened, what was approved, and what was spent. Every feature in the blueprint maps to at least one of these jobs. A feature that maps to none is cut.

### 2.4 Scope matrix

The scope matrix distinguishes what ships in the minimum viable product, what ships in the first expansion, and what is explicitly deferred. The minimum viable product includes the shell, the six agents, the thirteen-step reference mission, the payment gate, Twin Mode, Recorder, and persistence to Drive, Docs, Calendar, and Gmail. The first expansion adds custom agent creation, custom A2UI catalogs, team Spaces, and scheduled missions. Deferred work includes a native mobile shell, a marketplace, multi-user approval chains, and on-chain reputation. The deferrals are not a lack of ambition. They are a recognition that trust infrastructure must precede scale.

## 3. Experience Architecture: Shell, Spaces, Tabs, and Home

### 3.1 Browser shell

The shell is the user's persistent environment. It provides Spaces, pinned applications, tab search, split views, Focus and Zen modes, a breathing background, and a command palette. The shell is implemented as a side panel and an overlay, not as a replacement new-tab page alone, so that it remains available while the user works in any tab. The side panel is the primary host for the Mission Runner and the Agent Roster. The overlay hosts the command palette and the approval cards. The design respects the Chrome side panel model: a persistent home that stays visible while the user browses, with performance and memory management that avoids retaining unnecessary tab state.

The shell must meet strict performance budgets. Opening the side panel must feel instant, even when the service worker has been terminated and must restart. Tab search must return results in under 200 milliseconds for a typical profile. Split View must support one to four panes without reflowing the underlying pages in a way that breaks their layout. The breathing background must be implemented as a composited, GPU-friendly animation that can be disabled entirely in Settings, with a static fallback that preserves contrast. Accessibility is not an overlay. The shell must be navigable by keyboard, with visible focus, sufficient contrast, and screen-reader labels for every gate, cost, and agent name.

### 3.2 Spaces

Spaces separate contexts: work, research, personal, client, and study. Each Space carries its own pinned apps, tab groups, Home widgets, and mission history. Switching Spaces switches the visible tabs, the pinned apps, and the default Drive folder for Scribe artefacts. Spaces are not merely visual folders. They are scopes for consent and for cost. A mission started in a Work Space may not read files from a Personal Space without explicit re-authorisation. A mission's budget envelope is bound to its Space, so that a runaway mission in one context cannot consume the budget of another. The design follows the lesson of Arc, which rebuilt the browser around organisation but could not grow beyond power users because reorganising tabs was not a problem most people had [[4]](https://www.readsignal.io/article/ai-browser-war-comet-dia-atlas-arc-2026). Spaces in the Agents Edition exist to scope trust and cost, not to reorganise tabs for their own sake.

### 3.3 Pinned apps, tab search, and split view

Pinned apps provide one-click access to the tools the user uses most: Gmail, Drive, Docs, Calendar, Keep, Translate, Maps, and custom web apps. Pinned apps are per-Space and sync across devices through storage sync, with no credentials stored in the extension. Tab search indexes titles, URLs, and, with permission, the text of open tabs, and returns results ranked by recency and Space relevance. Split View allows the user to view one to four tabs side by side, with a drag handle to resize, a button to swap panes, and a button to promote any pane to full view. Split View is essential for comparison missions: the user can watch Scout results, Octo ranking, and the source page simultaneously, rather than toggling between tabs.

### 3.4 Focus, Zen, and breathing background

Focus mode dims all panes except the active one, hides the Home widgets, and batches notifications until the user exits Focus. Zen mode goes further: it hides the tab strip, the pinned apps, and the mission controls, leaving only the page and a single floating button to exit Zen. The breathing background is an ambient gradient that expands and contracts slowly, signalling that the system is idle, working, or awaiting approval through colour and motion, never through sound or interruption. The user may select from nine themes, Dark and Light modes, and a Breathing Light toggle. The themes affect only the shell chrome, never the content of web pages, and never the legibility of approval cards.

### 3.5 Home surface

Home is the default view when no mission is running. It presents a hero line, a weather widget, a calendar strip, a tasks list, Keep notes, Drive files, Translate, and Discover. Each widget is a read-only summary with a link to the underlying Google service. No widget performs a write without opening a mission. The weather widget uses a public weather source with the user's city, never precise location. The calendar strip shows the next three events with time, title, and attendees, and a button to create an event through a mission. The tasks list shows incomplete tasks with checkboxes that, when checked, create a mission to update the task rather than writing directly. The Drive widget shows recent files with type icons and a button to open in Drive. The Translate widget provides a text field and language picker that translates locally where possible and through the Translate service otherwise. Discover shows a small set of articles or tools relevant to the active Space, with a clear label that they are suggestions, not agent output.

### 3.6 Command palette and Google apps launcher

The command palette, invoked with Command-K or Control-K, provides fuzzy search across commands, Spaces, tabs, agents, missions, and Drive files. Every command shows its keyboard shortcut, its required scope, and whether it will open a gate. The Google apps launcher provides a grid of Google services with icons and names, opening each in a new tab. The launcher does not imply that the extension is a Google product. It is a convenience for services the user already uses, labelled as external links.


## 4. Agent System: Six Agents, Passports, DID, and Reputation

### 4.1 Roster and responsibilities

The roster contains six agents, each with a narrow, auditable responsibility. Narrowness is a security property. An agent that can do everything can be misled into doing anything. An agent that can only rank cannot pay. An agent that can only pay cannot draft. The roster is fixed in the minimum viable product. Custom agents are deferred to the first expansion, and when they arrive they must declare the same Passport fields and capability matrix.

**Nova Prime — Orchestrator.** Nova receives the user's outcome, decomposes it into steps, assigns steps to specialists, consolidates their outputs, and presents the plan and the final record. Nova never calls a payment tool, never writes to Drive, and never sends email. Nova's power is coordination, not execution. Its system prompt requires it to state assumptions, to ask for clarification when the outcome is ambiguous, and to refuse to proceed when a required scope is missing.

**Scout Fox — Search and Retrieval.** Scout searches the web, the user's tabs, and, with permission, Drive and Gmail. Scout returns cited evidence, with a URL, a timestamp, and a quoted excerpt for every claim. Scout does not rank, does not recommend, and does not summarise beyond extraction. Its output is a list of sources, not a conclusion.

**Professor Octo — Ranking and Synthesis.** Octo receives Scout's sources and a set of criteria from the user or from Nova, scores each candidate against each criterion, and produces a ranked table with a rationale for each score. Octo must disclose its criteria, its weights, and its uncertainty. A ranking without criteria is rejected by the validator.

**Sentinel Owl — Safety, Policy, and Spend.** Sentinel reviews every plan before execution, every payment before settlement, and every persistence step before writing. Sentinel checks the capability matrix, the spend limit, the scope grants, and a denylist of high-risk actions. Sentinel may halt a mission at any step, and its halt is final for that run. The reference mission demonstrates Sentinel halting at the 2.00 USDC checkpoint until the user approves. Sentinel's decisions are logged with the rule that triggered them.

**Bee Buyer — Payment via x402.** Bee is the only agent that may invoke a payment tool. Bee receives a payment requirement, verifies the amount, currency, destination, and network, and presents a payment card. Bee never initiates payment without a user click on that card. Bee verifies the settlement response and attaches the receipt to the mission record. Bee's spend limit is per mission and per day, and is enforced in the service worker, not in the prompt.

**Scribe Turtle — Persistence.** Scribe writes the mission record to Drive, creates a Doc, creates a Calendar event, and sends a summary email. Scribe receives a structured artefact bundle from Nova, validates its schema, and performs each write as a separate, idempotent operation with a retry policy and a deduplication key. Scribe never invents content. It persists what the mission produced.

### 4.2 Passports

Every agent carries a Passport that is visible in the roster and in every mission. The Passport contains the agent's display name, avatar, role, DID, model identifier, capability list, spend limit, reputation score, mission count, success rate, and a link to its audit history. The Passport is rendered as an A2UI card, not as free text, so that the client can validate its fields. Tapping a Passport opens a modal with the full audit history, including the last ten missions, their outcomes, and any Sentinel halts.

The Passport serves three functions. First, it makes identity legible. A user can distinguish Nova from a spoofed agent because the DID resolves to a known key. Second, it makes capability legible. A user can see that Scout cannot pay, and that Bee cannot draft, before delegating. Third, it makes history legible. A user can see that an agent has completed forty missions with a 96 percent success rate, or that it was halted twice for policy reasons, and can calibrate trust accordingly.

### 4.3 Decentralised identity

The system uses W3C Decentralised Identifiers for agent identity. A DID is an identifier of the form did:method:method-specific-id whose control is proven cryptographically, without a central issuing authority [[8]](https://www.agenid.com/compare/decentralized-identifiers). The W3C DID Core Recommendation, published on 19 July 2022, defines the model [[9]](https://www.agenid.com/glossary/decentralized-identifier). For the Agents Edition, the practical choice is did:key for local, ephemeral agents and did:web for stable, hosted agents. The DID Document contains the public key, the service endpoints, and the capability declarations. Verification is offline where possible: given the DID and a signature, any party can verify authenticity without contacting a registry.

The blueprint does not invent a new DID method. It uses existing methods and adds a narrow profile: every DID Document for an agent must contain a service entry for the mission endpoint, a verification method for the signing key, and a credential that binds the DID to the Passport's display name and role. Rotation is supported through key rotation in the DID Document, with a grace period during which both the old and new keys verify. Revocation is supported through a status list. An agent whose DID is revoked is immediately removed from the roster and cannot be assigned to a mission.

### 4.4 Reputation

Reputation is a score derived from observable behaviour, not from user ratings alone. The score combines mission success rate, Sentinel halt rate, cost accuracy, latency, and user approvals. A mission that completes within its stated cost and time improves the score. A mission that exceeds its cost envelope, that is halted by Sentinel, or that is denied by the user reduces it. Reputation is displayed as a score out of 100, with a sparkline of the last twenty missions and a breakdown of the components. Reputation never grants additional capability. A high-reputation agent cannot exceed its spend limit. Reputation informs the user's decision to delegate, not the system's enforcement.

### 4.5 Capability matrix

The capability matrix is the enforcement table that the service worker consults before every tool call. It lists each agent, each tool, and whether the agent may call it, and under what conditions. Nova may call plan, assign, and consolidate, but may not call pay, write, or send. Scout may call search, fetch, and list, but may not call rank, pay, or write. Octo may call rank and synthesise, but may not call search, pay, or write. Sentinel may call check, halt, and audit, but may not call pay or write. Bee may call pay and verify, but may not call search, rank, or write. Scribe may call write, create, and send, but may not call search, rank, or pay. The matrix is stored as a signed JSON file in the extension bundle, versioned, and verified at startup. A prompt that asks an agent to exceed its matrix is rejected by the validator before any tool is invoked.

### 4.6 Prompting and grounding

Each agent's system prompt is versioned, stored in the bundle, and disclosed in the Passport. The prompt states the agent's role, its capability matrix, its output schema, and its refusal behaviour. Grounding is provided through Gemini with Google Search, with the understanding that grounding queries are metered and that the system must cache grounding results per mission. The agents do not receive raw credentials, API keys, or tokens. They receive tool interfaces that the service worker implements. A model that is compromised through prompt injection cannot exfiltrate a token it never possessed.

## 5. Mission Runner: Thirteen-Step Reference Mission, State Machine, and Gates

### 5.1 Mission as a first-class object

A mission is a structured object with an identifier, a Space, a user outcome, a plan, a sequence of steps, a budget envelope, a gate list, a cost ledger, and an artefact list. The mission object is persisted in IndexedDB and synced to Drive on completion. The mission object is the unit of audit, of billing, and of user understanding. A chat transcript is not a mission. A mission has a beginning, a middle, an end, and a record.

### 5.2 The thirteen-step reference mission

The reference mission that the prototype implements and that this blueprint standardises is the evaluation of a Model Context Protocol gateway candidate, pg-mcp-gateway, with a capped payment, a ranking, and persistence to Workspace. The thirteen steps are as follows.

Step 1, Scout searches for pg-mcp-gateway across the web and the user's tabs, returning cited sources. Step 2, Octo extracts the candidate's capabilities, protocol version, and maintenance signals. Step 3, Octo scores the candidate against criteria supplied by the user: protocol compliance, documentation quality, maintenance, and cost. Step 4, Nova consolidates the ranking into a single table with evidence links. Step 5, Sentinel reviews the plan, the scopes, and the spend limit, and approves continuation. Step 6, Nova presents the plan and the budget envelope, and the user approves continuation. Step 7, Bee requests the paid resource, receives an HTTP 402 response with a PAYMENT-REQUIRED header, and presents a payment card for 2.00 USDC. Step 8, Sentinel halts at the checkpoint, verifies amount, currency, destination, and network, and awaits user approval. Step 9, the user approves, Bee signs the payment, retries with PAYMENT-SIGNATURE, and receives PAYMENT-RESPONSE. Step 10, Bee verifies settlement and attaches the receipt. Step 11, Scribe writes the mission record to Drive. Step 12, Scribe creates a Doc with the ranking and evidence, creates a Calendar event for the follow-up, and sends a summary email. Step 13, Nova presents the final record with links to the Drive file, the Doc, the Calendar event, and the email, and the Recorder marks the mission complete.

Each step has a status: pending, running, awaiting approval, approved, denied, completed, failed, or halted. Each step has a start time, an end time, a cost, and a log. The user may rewind to any completed step and re-run from there, with the system invalidating downstream steps. The user may deny any gate, which marks the mission denied and stops all further steps with no funds moved beyond those already settled.

### 5.3 State machine

The mission state machine is explicit and testable. A mission begins in DRAFT. When Nova produces a plan, it moves to PLANNED. When the user approves the plan, it moves to RUNNING. When a gate is encountered, it moves to AWAITING_APPROVAL. When the user approves, it returns to RUNNING. When the user denies, it moves to DENIED. When Sentinel halts, it moves to HALTED, from which the user may resume, edit the plan, or abort. When all steps complete, it moves to COMPLETED. When a step fails and cannot be retried, it moves to FAILED, with an error record and a suggested remediation. The state machine is implemented in the service worker with a transition table, not with ad hoc conditionals, and every transition is logged.

### 5.4 Human gates

Three gates are mandatory in the reference mission and in every mission that includes payment or persistence. The plan gate requires the user to approve the plan, the budget, and the scope list before any execution. The payment gate requires the user to approve the amount, currency, destination, and network before Bee signs. The persistence gate requires the user to approve the artefacts to be written, their destinations, and their sharing settings before Scribe writes. Each gate is rendered as an A2UI card with the exact values, not as a generic confirmation dialog. The card shows the cost, the destination, and the consequence of approval. The user may edit the plan at any gate, which returns the mission to DRAFT with the edits applied.

### 5.5 Twin Mode

Twin Mode is a dry-run simulation of a mission with no funds moved, no writes performed, and no emails sent. Twin Mode executes the same plan, with the same agents, against mocked tools that return realistic fixtures. The user can compare the Twin run with the live run, and can promote a Twin plan to a live mission with a single approval. Twin Mode is the primary tool for the builder persona and for training new users. It is also the primary tool for testing: every release must pass the Twin suite for the reference mission before shipping.

### 5.6 Recorder

Recorder is the flight log. It records every step, every tool call, every gate, every approval, and every receipt, with timestamps. The Recorder provides a timeline scrubber that allows the user to rewind to any step and inspect the state at that moment. The Recorder exports a JSON file and a human-readable HTML report. The Recorder is not a debug log. It is a user-facing evidence surface, with plain language for each entry, and with technical details available on expansion. The Recorder's data is stored locally and, on mission completion, written to Drive as part of the mission record.

## 6. Protocol Layer: MCP, A2UI, A2A, x402, and OAuth

### 6.1 Model Context Protocol

The Model Context Protocol is an open standard that defines how AI applications discover and call external tools, read data, and load prompt templates through one JSON-RPC 2.0 interface [[10]](https://www.bdemerson.com/article/model-context-protocol). The current specification revision, 2026-07-28, made the protocol stateless, deprecated Roots, Sampling, and Logging, and moved long-running tasks into an optional extension. The Agents Edition uses MCP for tool discovery and invocation. Each MCP server exposes tools, resources, and prompts. The extension acts as an MCP host, instantiating one client per server connection. The pg-mcp-gateway candidate in the reference mission is evaluated precisely because it speaks this protocol.

MCP authorisation follows OAuth 2.1. Under the 2026-07-28 revision, an MCP server must implement OAuth 2.0 Protected Resource Metadata, a client must send the resource parameter on authorisation and token requests, and the server must reject any token not issued for itself. Dynamic Client Registration is deprecated in favour of Client ID Metadata Documents. The extension implements the full flow, including PKCE with S256, issuer validation, and step-up through 403 insufficient_scope. A server that does not implement the required metadata is not added to the roster.

### 6.2 Agent-to-User Interface

A2UI is the protocol for rendering agent output as interactive interfaces. The core idea is that the agent never sends markup. It sends a stream of small JSON messages describing components and a separate data model those components bind to, and the client renders with its own trusted components [[7]](https://mikezupper.com/posts/a2ui-agent-to-user-interface-protocol/). Four message types cover the protocol: createSurface, updateComponents, updateDataModel, and deleteSurface, with client-to-server action and error messages closing the loop. The component tree is a flat list, not a tree, which allows streaming: a parent can reference a child that has not arrived yet, and the client renders a placeholder until it arrives.

The Agents Edition uses A2UI for every interactive surface: approval cards, ranking tables, payment cards, Passport modals, and the Mission Runner. The extension defines a custom catalog scoped to its design system, with components for AgentCard, MissionStep, PaymentCard, RankingTable, PassportModal, and RecorderTimeline. The catalog is baked into the agents' prompts, so that an agent cannot request a component outside the catalog. The client validates every A2UI message against the catalog schema and returns errors to the agent for self-correction. Sensitive values never reach the model: A2UI binds to data on the server, so that a payment amount or a file identifier is resolved at render time, not in the model's context.

A2UI 1.0 adds bidirectional function calls, so that a picker can check availability on the spot, and adds accessibility and extensions [[6]](https://developers.googleblog.com/a2ui-10-build-delightful-fast-reliable-agentic-experiences/). The blueprint adopts 1.0 as the target, with a fallback to 0.9.1 for hosts that have not yet upgraded, including Gemini Enterprise, which was being upgraded to 1.0 at the time of the announcement. The extension detects the host's A2UI version through the A2A extension negotiation header and adapts its message format accordingly.

### 6.3 Agent-to-Agent and MCP Apps

A2UI does not care about transport. It rides A2A, MCP, WebSocket, REST, or Server-Sent Events [[7]](https://mikezupper.com/posts/a2ui-agent-to-user-interface-protocol/). Each message travels as an A2A DataPart tagged application/a2ui+json, and A2UI is advertised as an A2A extension negotiated via the X-A2A-Extensions header. MCP Apps provide a complementary path: an MCP server returns an interactive interface that the host renders in a sandboxed iframe. A2UI can run inside an MCP App, with the MCP App containing a web renderer that turns A2UI messages into an interface within the host's isolated frame. The Agents Edition supports both paths: A2UI over A2A for native surfaces, and A2UI inside MCP Apps for third-party agents that expose MCP App interfaces. The security model is the same in both cases: the host renders, the agent describes, and no agent-supplied code executes.

### 6.4 x402 payments

x402 is an open HTTP 402 payment protocol that lets servers charge AI agents and machines for API calls using stablecoins [[11]](https://eco.com/support/en/articles/14839402-x402-protocol-explained?ref=blockhead.co). Coinbase released x402 on 6 May 2025, and the x402 Foundation became operational on 14 July 2026. The protocol revives the dormant HTTP 402 Payment Required status code. A server answers an unpaid request with 402 and payment instructions; the client retries with a signed stablecoin payment in a header; the server verifies and releases the resource. Payments are most commonly made in USDC, with Base as the main settlement chain.

x402 v2 uses three standardised headers for payment communication: PAYMENT-REQUIRED from server to client, PAYMENT-SIGNATURE from client to server, and PAYMENT-RESPONSE from server to client [[12]](https://docs.x402.org/core-concepts/http-402). All headers contain Base64-encoded JSON. PAYMENT-REQUIRED contains the payment requirements, including accepted schemes, price, network, and destination. PAYMENT-SIGNATURE contains the signed payment payload. PAYMENT-RESPONSE contains the settlement response, whether successful or failed. The v2 specification, launched on 11 December 2025, renamed the headers, moved network identifiers to CAIP-2, and added multi-facilitator support and an extensions framework.

The Agents Edition implements x402 v2 through Bee Buyer, with strict constraints. Bee never holds a private key in the extension's JavaScript context. Signing is performed through the user's wallet or through a delegated signing service that the user has authorised, with the private key remaining in the wallet. Bee verifies the PAYMENT-REQUIRED envelope, checks that the amount matches the budget envelope, that the currency is USDC, that the network is an allowed network, and that the destination matches the expected recipient for the resource. Bee presents the payment card with the exact amount and destination, and only signs after the user clicks approve. Bee verifies the PAYMENT-RESPONSE, checks the settlement transaction, and attaches the receipt. A failed settlement is retried at most once, with a new payment requirement, and is logged as a failure. The system never retries a payment silently.

### 6.5 OAuth and granular permissions

Google OAuth is the identity and authorisation layer for Workspace integration. Granular permissions give users more control over the specific data they share, improving transparency, security, and trust [[3]](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/resources/granular-permissions). Applications requesting only sign-in scopes or only one non-sign-in scope are not subject to the granular consent screen. Applications requesting both sign-in and non-sign-in scopes, or more than one non-sign-in scope, display a granular consent screen where the user can select which permissions to approve. The extension must handle the case where the user grants only some scopes.

The blueprint specifies incremental authorisation as the default. At first run, the extension requests only sign-in scopes: openid, email, profile. When the user starts a mission that requires Drive, the extension requests drive.file, with an explanation of why. When the mission requires Gmail, it requests gmail.send and gmail.readonly, with a separate explanation. When it requires Calendar, it requests calendar.events. The extension uses the include_granted_scopes parameter to preserve previously granted scopes, and checks granted scopes before every step. If a scope is missing, the step is disabled with a message that names the scope and offers to request it. The extension never assumes that all requested scopes were granted.

The Chrome Identity API is used for the extension's OAuth flow, in accordance with the granular permissions guidance for Chrome extensions. Tokens are stored in chrome.storage.session, never in localStorage, never in a content script, and never in a URL. Refresh tokens are stored in chrome.storage.local with encryption at rest provided by the platform. Token scope, expiry, and audience are validated before every API call. A token that fails audience validation is discarded and re-requested.


## 7. Browser Platform: Manifest V3, Service Worker, Offscreen, and Side Panel

### 7.1 Manifest V3

Manifest V3 replaces persistent background pages with event-driven service workers, and that single decision ripples through every other architectural choice [[13]](https://www.mv3-extension.com/manifest-v3-architecture-extension-lifecycle/). The extension's manifest declares version 3, a service worker as the background context, host permissions for the sites it may access, web accessible resources for the side panel and A2UI renderer, and an offscreen document for DOM-dependent work. The migration checklist for Manifest V3 requires updating the manifest version, updating host permissions, updating web accessible resources, migrating to a service worker, updating API calls, replacing blocking web request listeners with declarative net request rules, and improving security by removing remotely hosted code and execution of arbitrary strings [[2]](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/extensions/develop/migrate/checklist). The Agents Edition is built as a Manifest V3 extension from the outset, not as a migrated Manifest V2 extension, and therefore avoids the migration debt that many extensions carry.

### 7.2 Service worker lifecycle

The service worker is ephemeral. Chrome suspends it after roughly thirty seconds of inactivity and can terminate it at any time, and terminates it when a single request takes longer than five minutes. The architecture must therefore treat termination as normal, not as an error. Every mission step is persisted to IndexedDB before it begins, and the service worker restores its state on restart by reading the mission object. Long-running work, such as grounding, ranking, or payment verification, is broken into chunks that each complete within the worker's lifetime, with progress persisted between chunks. Timers are replaced with alarms. Listeners are registered synchronously at the top level of the worker, not inside asynchronous callbacks, so that they survive restart. State is persisted, not held in memory.

The service worker handles extension-wide logic and privileged browser APIs, but cannot touch the page DOM directly. Content scripts handle DOM access, with a narrow message interface to the service worker. The service worker never trusts a message from a content script without validating its origin, its schema, and its permission. A content script that is compromised through a malicious page cannot escalate to a payment or a write, because the service worker enforces the capability matrix independently of the content script's claims.

### 7.3 Offscreen documents

The service worker has no document, no DOMParser, no Audio, and no navigator.clipboard. The offscreen document is the sanctioned replacement: a hidden extension page that the worker creates on demand, talks to over chrome.runtime messaging, and closes when done. The Agents Edition uses the offscreen document for three tasks: DOM parsing of fetched pages, clipboard read and write with the CLIPBOARD reason, and WebAssembly or heavy compute that cannot run in the single-threaded service worker. The offscreen document supports only the chrome.runtime API, and the extension respects that limitation. Only one offscreen document may exist at a time, and the extension creates it lazily, reuses it across tasks, and closes it when idle. The offscreen document never receives credentials, tokens, or payment keys. It receives data to parse and returns structured results.

### 7.4 Side panel, tabs, and scripting

The side panel hosts the Mission Runner, the Agent Roster, and the Home surface. The tabs API provides tab-level control, with throttling of onUpdated listeners and batching of queries to avoid performance degradation. The scripting API replaces the deprecated tabs.executeScript and tabs.insertCSS, with explicit host permissions and user activation where required. The declarativeNetRequest API replaces blocking web request listeners, with rules that describe actions to perform when conditions are met, rather than code that reads and alters network requests programmatically [[2]](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/extensions/develop/migrate/checklist). The extension does not block, redirect, or modify network requests except to add the PAYMENT-SIGNATURE header to an x402 retry, which is performed by the service worker through fetch, not through a blocking listener.

### 7.5 Storage, alarms, and messaging

Storage is divided by purpose. chrome.storage.session holds tokens and ephemeral mission state. chrome.storage.local holds settings, themes, Spaces, and pinned apps. IndexedDB holds mission records, Recorder logs, and cached grounding results. chrome.storage.sync holds only the minimal data needed to sync Spaces and settings across devices, never tokens or mission content. Alarms replace timers for scheduled missions, for token refresh, and for budget checks. Messaging between contexts uses chrome.runtime.sendMessage with a versioned envelope that includes a message type, a mission identifier, a sequence number, and a signature. Messages without a valid envelope are dropped and logged.

### 7.6 Performance budgets

The extension must meet explicit budgets. Side panel open must complete in under 300 milliseconds when warm and under 800 milliseconds when cold. Tab search must return in under 200 milliseconds. Mission step start must complete in under 500 milliseconds, excluding model latency. Memory usage must remain under 150 megabytes for a typical profile with twenty tabs. The breathing background must run at 60 frames per second on integrated graphics, or degrade to a static gradient. The extension must not register a persistent content script on every page. Content scripts are injected on demand, for the active tab, for the duration of a mission step, and removed when the step completes.

## 8. Google Workspace Integration: Gmail, Drive, Docs, Calendar, Translate, and Keep

### 8.1 Integration philosophy

Workspace integration is the reason the Agents Edition is valuable to a Google user, and the reason it must be conservative. The extension reads and writes Workspace data only through official APIs, only with the scopes the user granted, and only within a mission that the user approved. It does not scrape Gmail or Drive through the DOM. It does not store Workspace content in the extension beyond the mission record. It respects the user's sharing settings and never changes them without explicit approval. Every write is idempotent, with a deduplication key derived from the mission identifier and the step identifier, so that a retry after a service worker restart does not create a duplicate Doc, event, or email.

### 8.2 Gmail

Gmail integration provides search, read, draft, and send. Search uses the Gmail API with a query constructed from the mission's criteria, with results limited to the number the user requested. Read returns the message headers, the body, and the attachments metadata, never the full attachment content unless the user explicitly requests it. Draft creates a draft with the mission's content, and presents it for review before sending. Send is a gated action that requires the persistence gate. The extension never sends email without the user approving the recipient, the subject, and the body. The summary email in the reference mission is a Scribe artefact, with the recipient, subject, and body shown in the persistence gate before sending.

### 8.3 Drive and Docs

Drive integration provides file listing, file read, file creation, and file sharing metadata. The extension requests the drive.file scope, which grants access only to files the user opens or creates with the extension, rather than the full drive scope, which would grant access to all files. The mission record is written as a JSON file in a folder named for the Space, with a filename derived from the mission identifier and timestamp. The Doc is created through the Docs API with the ranking, evidence, and receipts, and is shared only as the user specified in the persistence gate. The default sharing is private. The user may change it to link-shared or to a specific collaborator, with the change shown in the gate. The extension never makes a file public without explicit approval.

### 8.4 Calendar

Calendar integration provides event listing, event creation, and event update. The calendar strip on Home shows the next three events, with time, title, and attendees, and a button to create an event through a mission. Event creation in the reference mission creates a follow-up event with the mission title, the artefact links in the description, and the attendees the user specified. The extension requests calendar.events, not full calendar access, and respects the user's working hours and free-busy settings where the API provides them. A mission that creates an event must show the event's time, duration, attendees, and location in the persistence gate before creating it.

### 8.5 Translate and Keep

Translate provides a text field and language picker on Home, with translation through the Translate service where local translation is unavailable. Keep provides a notes widget that shows the user's recent notes and allows the user to create a note through a mission. Keep notes are read-only in the widget; editing opens Keep in a new tab. The design avoids duplicating Keep's functionality in the extension, and instead provides a summary with a link to the source. The same principle applies to every Workspace widget: the extension summarises, links, and delegates, and does not reimplement the service.

### 8.6 Quotas, caching, and backoff

Workspace APIs impose quotas that punish naive polling. The extension therefore caches file listings, calendar events, and Gmail search results per mission, with a time-to-live of five minutes, and invalidates the cache on write. It batches API calls where the API supports batching, and uses exponential backoff with jitter on 429 and 500 responses. It respects Retry-After headers. It logs every API call with its latency and quota cost in the mission record, so that the user can see why a mission was slow or expensive. A mission that exceeds its quota budget is halted by Sentinel, with a message that names the quota and suggests a retry time.

## 9. Safety, Human Gates, Twin Mode, and Recorder

### 9.1 Threat model

The threat model assumes that the model can be misled, that web content can contain hidden instructions, and that a third-party MCP server can be malicious. Prompt injection is ranked as the top risk in the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications, as LLM01, in both the 2025 and 2026 editions, and OWASP states that it is unclear whether foolproof prevention exists [[14]](https://www.surehand.io/articles/indirect-prompt-injection-through-documents). Indirect injection is the danger for agents: the attacker plants instructions in a web page, a document, or an email, and waits for the agent to read it. The Agents Edition therefore treats all ingested content as untrusted data, never as instructions, and enforces the capability matrix in the service worker, not in the model.

The threat model also includes excessive agency, in which an agent with too many tools or too broad permissions causes harm, and insecure output handling, in which agent output is rendered without validation. The A2UI catalog and the capability matrix address both: the agent cannot request a component outside the catalog, and cannot call a tool outside its matrix, regardless of what a prompt injection attempt instructs it to do. The system also addresses tool misuse through Sentinel, which reviews every plan and every payment against a denylist of high-risk actions, including credential entry, account deletion, bulk sharing, and payment above the spend limit.

### 9.2 Defence in depth

Defence is layered. At the model layer, system prompts state the agent's role, its matrix, and its refusal behaviour, and ingested content is delimited with explicit markers that label it as data. At the validator layer, every A2UI message, every tool call, and every mission transition is validated against a schema before execution. At the service worker layer, the capability matrix, the spend limit, and the scope grants are enforced independently of the model's output. At the human layer, gates require explicit approval for consequential actions. At the audit layer, the Recorder logs every decision, so that a failure can be investigated after the fact. No single layer is trusted. A failure in one layer must be caught by the next.

### 9.3 Human gates in detail

The plan gate, the payment gate, and the persistence gate are described in Section 5.4. This section adds implementation detail. Each gate is rendered as an A2UI card with a unique gate identifier, a mission identifier, a timestamp, and a list of fields with their exact values. The gate card includes a primary approve button, a secondary edit button, and a deny button. The gate card is not dismissible by clicking outside it. The gate remains open until the user acts, and the mission remains in AWAITING_APPROVAL. The gate's decision is signed with the user's session key and stored in the mission record. A gate that is approved by a script, by a content script, or by any means other than a user click on the card is rejected by the validator.

### 9.4 Twin Mode and Recorder in detail

Twin Mode and Recorder are described in Sections 5.5 and 5.6. This section adds the testing implication: Twin Mode is the test harness. Every release must pass the Twin suite, which runs the reference mission against fixtures, asserts that the plan contains thirteen steps, that Sentinel halts at the payment checkpoint, that Bee does not sign without approval, that Scribe does not write without approval, and that the final record contains links to the Drive file, the Doc, the Calendar event, and the email. The Recorder suite asserts that every step is logged, that every gate is logged, and that the export contains the same data as the local record. A release that fails either suite does not ship.

### 9.5 Incident response

When a mission fails, is halted, or is denied, the system presents a clear explanation, a suggested remediation, and a button to rewind, edit, or abort. The explanation names the rule that triggered the halt, the agent that was involved, and the cost incurred. The remediation suggests a concrete next step, such as reducing the spend limit, granting a scope, or editing the plan. The incident is logged in the Recorder and, if the user consents, in an anonymised telemetry event that helps the team improve the validator. The system never blames the user for a halt. A halt is a success of the safety system, not a failure of the user.

## 10. Privacy, Compliance, Brand, and Liability

### 10.1 Privacy by design

Privacy is a design property, not a policy document. The extension collects only the data required for the mission, retains it only for the mission's lifetime plus the user's retention setting, and deletes it on request. Tokens are never logged. File content is never logged. Email bodies are never logged beyond the headers required for the mission record. Grounding queries are logged as counts, not as content. The extension does not train models on user data. It does not sell user data. It does not share user data with third-party MCP servers beyond the data required for the tool call, and it discloses that data in the plan gate. The privacy notice is written in plain language, with a table that lists each data type, its purpose, its retention, and its deletion path.

### 10.2 GDPR and Article 22

The General Data Protection Regulation applies to processing of personal data of persons in the European Union. Article 22 provides that a data subject has the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning the data subject or similarly significantly affects the data subject. The European Data Protection Board's guidelines require that human review be meaningful, performed by someone with the authority and competence to alter the decision, not someone whose role is to approve in batches. The Agents Edition therefore never performs a solely automated decision with legal or similarly significant effects. Every consequential decision requires a human gate, with a reviewer who can alter the decision. The system provides a right to human review, to express a point of view, and to contest the outcome, as required where an exception applies. A data protection impact assessment is required before shipping any feature that profiles users or makes automated decisions, and the assessment is reviewed on every major release.

### 10.3 EU AI Act and Article 50

The EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, entered enforcement on 2 August 2026, with Article 50 transparency duties applicable from that date, and with high-risk obligations for stand-alone Annex III systems deferred to 2 December 2027 under the Digital Omnibus on AI [[15]](https://dev.orithos.com/research/eu-ai-act-what-applies-now). Article 50 requires that a deployer of an AI system that interacts with humans make the AI interaction clear, and that synthetic content be identifiable. The Agents Edition complies by labelling every agent output as agent output, by disclosing the agent's identity in the Passport, and by marking synthetic content, including generated Docs and emails, as AI-generated in the artefact itself. Prohibited practices, banned since 2 February 2025, including social scoring and manipulative techniques that cause harm, are excluded by the capability matrix and by Sentinel's denylist. The system maintains a risk management process, logging, and human oversight that would satisfy the high-risk obligations if the system were classified as high-risk, even where the current classification does not require it. The evidence pack is generated as a byproduct of testing, not as a separate track: test inventory, findings log, remediation trail, and residual posture are all produced by the Recorder and the test suite.

### 10.4 CCPA and CPRA automated decision-making

The California Privacy Protection Agency finalised regulations under the California Consumer Privacy Act on 24 July 2025, introducing obligations for automated decision-making technology, expected to take effect beginning 1 January 2026, with full compliance required by 1 January 2027 for businesses already using such technology for significant decisions. The regulations require pre-use notice and, in most cases, an opt-out right when a business uses automated decision-making technology to make a significant decision, and access to information about how the technology is used. The Agents Edition complies by providing pre-use notice in the plan gate, which names the automated processing, its purpose, and its logic, by providing an opt-out that disables the automated decision and routes the task to a manual flow, and by providing access to the mission record, which contains the logic, the data, and the outcome. The system honours Global Privacy Control signals and the California Opt Me Out Act's browser-level opt-out signal, and provides a privacy dashboard where the user can exercise all CCPA rights.

### 10.5 Brand and trademark

Google brand guidance prohibits using Google trademarks in the product's own name, domain, or slogan, prohibits implying endorsement, affiliation, or sponsorship where none exists, and prohibits imitating Google's logo or visual identity [[1]](https://about.google/brand-resource-center/guidance/?authuser=2&hl=es-419). The product may refer to Google or its products in plain text in an informational context, as long as it follows trademark rules. The Agents Edition therefore uses a distinct name, domain, and visual identity, with no Google logo, no Google colour combination, and no Google product icon in its own branding. The extension's listing, website, and in-product copy state clearly that it is an independent product that works with Google services, not a Google product. The Google apps launcher uses plain text names and generic icons, not Google product icons, unless a specific icon usage guideline permits it and a request has been submitted. The team must not submit a request asking for permission to do any of the prohibited uses, because those uses are not permitted. The brand review is part of the release checklist, with a screenshot review of every surface that mentions Google.

### 10.6 Liability and terms

The terms of service state clearly that the user is responsible for reviewing agent output before approval, that the extension is provided as is, and that the provider's liability is limited as permitted by law. The terms do not disclaim liability for the provider's own negligence, and do not purport to waive rights that cannot be waived under consumer protection law. The terms disclose the x402 payment flow, the wallet requirement, the irreversibility of on-chain settlement, and the user's responsibility to verify the destination before approving. The terms disclose that the extension does not provide legal, financial, or medical advice, and that agent output must not be relied upon for such decisions without independent verification. The terms are versioned, with a changelog, and the user is notified of material changes before they take effect. The privacy notice and terms are reviewed by counsel before every major release.


## 11. Monetization, Economics, Quotas, and Cost Model

### 11.1 Monetization model

Chrome Web Store payments were deprecated, with no new paid extensions or in-app items from 21 September 2020 and no charges after 1 February 2021. Paid extensions therefore use their own checkout through a provider such as Stripe, Paddle, or a similar service, with the server telling the extension who paid, and must disclose in the listing if basic functionality requires payment and identify the seller as the developer, not Google. The Agents Edition follows this model. The extension itself is free to install, with a free tier that includes the shell, the six agents, and a limited number of missions per month. A paid tier, billed through an external checkout, provides higher mission limits, higher spend limits, custom Spaces, team features, and priority grounding. The listing states clearly that the seller is the developer, not Google, and that payment is processed externally.

The free tier is not a trial that silently converts. The user is notified when approaching the free limit, and the mission is paused, not charged, at the limit. The paid tier is billed per seat per month, with a clear cancellation path. Enterprise billing provides a single invoice, a central admin console, and a data processing agreement. The system never charges for a mission without the user's explicit approval of the mission's budget envelope, which includes the subscription cost, the grounding cost, and the x402 settlement cost, presented separately.

### 11.2 Cost model

Every mission has a budget envelope with three components. The model cost is the token cost for the agents' reasoning, estimated from the plan's length and the agents' history, and measured from the actual token usage after execution. The grounding cost is the cost of Google Search grounding queries, metered per query with a free allowance and a per-thousand price beyond it, estimated from the number of searches in the plan. The settlement cost is the x402 amount plus any network fees, stated exactly in the payment card. The envelope is presented in the plan gate, with a breakdown and a total. A mission that exceeds its envelope by more than ten percent is halted by Sentinel, with a message that names the overrun and offers to increase the envelope or abort. The cost ledger is part of the mission record, with each step's cost, the cumulative cost, and the remaining budget.

### 11.3 Quotas and rate limits

The extension respects quotas for Gemini, Workspace, and x402. Gemini grounding is cached per mission, with a time-to-live of one hour, and duplicate queries are deduplicated. Workspace API calls are batched and cached as described in Section 8.6. x402 calls are limited to one per payment requirement, with a single retry on failure. The extension implements a token bucket for each API, with a refill rate derived from the documented quota, and queues requests when the bucket is empty, rather than failing. The user is shown the queue depth and the estimated wait time. A mission that is queued for more than five minutes is offered the option to continue in the background, with a notification on completion.

### 11.4 Unit economics

The unit economics are stated explicitly, not as an aspiration. A typical reference mission consumes approximately eight thousand tokens for planning and ranking, three grounding queries, and one x402 settlement of 2.00 USDC. The model's token cost, the grounding cost, and the settlement cost together define the marginal cost of the mission. The subscription price must exceed the marginal cost of the median user's monthly missions by a factor that covers fixed costs, support, and a margin. The free tier's mission limit is set so that the median free user's marginal cost is covered by the conversion rate to paid, not by advertising or data sale. The team reviews the unit economics monthly, with a dashboard that shows marginal cost per mission, conversion rate, and churn, and adjusts the free limit if the economics deteriorate. The dashboard is not public, but its methodology is disclosed in the terms.

## 12. Engineering Plan: Repository, Testing, Observability, and Release

### 12.1 Repository and modules

The repository is a monorepo with packages for the extension, the A2UI renderer, the agent prompts, the MCP client, the x402 client, the DID verifier, and the Recorder. Each package has its own tests, its own version, and its own changelog. The extension package contains the manifest, the service worker, the side panel, the content scripts, and the offscreen document. The A2UI renderer package contains the catalog, the validator, and the React, Lit, and Flutter renderers. The agent prompts package contains the versioned system prompts for each agent, with a test that asserts that each prompt contains the capability matrix and the refusal behaviour. The MCP client package contains the JSON-RPC 2.0 client, the OAuth flow, and the stateless transport. The x402 client package contains the header parsing, the signature verification, and the settlement verification. The DID verifier package contains the DID resolution, the key verification, and the revocation check. The Recorder package contains the mission object, the state machine, and the export logic.

### 12.2 Testing

Testing is layered. Unit tests cover the validator, the state machine, the capability matrix, the cost ledger, and the DID verifier, with a coverage target of ninety percent for those modules. Integration tests cover the MCP client against a mock server, the x402 client against a mock facilitator, and the Workspace clients against recorded fixtures. End-to-end tests cover the reference mission in Twin Mode, with assertions on the thirteen steps, the gates, the payment, and the artefacts. Adversarial tests cover prompt injection, indirect injection through a malicious page, tool misuse, and scope escalation, with a suite that is updated on every release. Accessibility tests cover keyboard navigation, focus, contrast, and screen-reader labels. Performance tests cover the budgets in Section 7.6, with a failure threshold that blocks release. The test suite runs on every pull request, and the full suite, including the adversarial suite, runs before every release.

### 12.3 Observability

Observability is built into the mission record, not added as an afterthought. Every mission step logs its start time, end time, cost, tool calls, and outcome. Every gate logs its decision, its timestamp, and its signer. Every payment logs its requirement, its signature, and its settlement. The extension exposes a local dashboard that shows mission count, success rate, cost, and halt rate, with no data leaving the device unless the user consents to anonymised telemetry. Anonymised telemetry, when consented, contains counts and latencies, never content, never tokens, never file names, and never email addresses. The telemetry pipeline is documented, with a data retention period of ninety days and a deletion path.

### 12.4 Release process

Release follows a staged process. A pull request requires two reviews, one from a peer engineer and one from a reviewer who did not write the code, with a checklist that includes brand review, privacy review, and security review. A release candidate is built, signed, and tested in Twin Mode against the reference mission. A canary release is deployed to five percent of users, with monitoring for error rate, halt rate, and cost overrun. A full release is deployed only after the canary has run for forty-eight hours without a critical incident. A rollback is prepared before every release, with a single command to revert to the previous version. The release notes list every change, every new scope, every new capability, and every known issue. The user is notified of a release that adds a scope, with a prompt to grant or deny it.

### 12.5 Support and documentation

Documentation is part of the product. The user guide explains Spaces, missions, gates, Passports, Twin Mode, and Recorder in plain language, with screenshots and a video walkthrough of the reference mission. The developer guide explains the MCP client, the A2UI catalog, the x402 flow, and the DID verifier, with code samples and a local development setup. The compliance guide explains GDPR Article 22, EU AI Act Article 50, CCPA automated decision-making, and brand guidance, with a checklist for each. Support is provided through a help centre, with a response time target of one business day for paid users and three business days for free users. Support staff have no access to user tokens, file content, or mission content without the user's explicit consent for a specific mission.

## 13. Roadmap, Milestones, KPIs, and Risks

### 13.1 Roadmap

The roadmap is divided into four phases. Phase 0, Foundation, delivers the shell, the side panel, Spaces, tab search, split view, Focus and Zen modes, and the Home surface, with no agents. Phase 1, Agents, delivers the six agents, Passports, the capability matrix, and the Mission Runner with the thirteen-step reference mission, Twin Mode, and Recorder. Phase 2, Payments and Persistence, delivers x402 payments, Drive, Docs, Calendar, and Gmail persistence, with the three gates and the cost ledger. Phase 3, Expansion, delivers custom agents, custom A2UI catalogs, team Spaces, scheduled missions, and enterprise billing. Each phase has a defined exit criterion: Phase 0 exits when the shell meets the performance budgets, Phase 1 exits when the Twin suite passes, Phase 2 exits when the live reference mission completes with a receipt and artefacts, and Phase 3 exits when the first team pilot completes ten missions without a Sentinel failure.

### 13.2 Milestones

Milestone 1, Shell Complete, is the browser shell with Spaces, pinned apps, tab search, split view, and Home, with no agents, delivered in four weeks. Milestone 2, Agents Complete, is the six agents with Passports and the Mission Runner in Twin Mode, delivered in eight weeks. Milestone 3, Reference Mission Live, is the thirteen-step mission with payment and persistence, delivered in twelve weeks. Milestone 4, Compliance Review, is a review of GDPR, EU AI Act, CCPA, and brand, with a data protection impact assessment and a brand screenshot review, delivered in fourteen weeks. Milestone 5, Public Beta, is a beta release to one thousand users, with monitoring and support, delivered in sixteen weeks. Milestone 6, General Availability, is a public release with paid billing, delivered in twenty weeks. The milestones are sequential, with no overlap that would allow a later phase to begin before the earlier phase's exit criterion is met.

### 13.3 Key performance indicators

The product tracks five key performance indicators. Mission success rate is the percentage of missions that complete without failure, with a target of ninety percent. Gate approval rate is the percentage of gates approved, with a target that is not maximised: a high approval rate may indicate that gates are not meaningful, and a low rate may indicate that plans are poor. Cost accuracy is the percentage of missions that complete within ten percent of their budget envelope, with a target of ninety-five percent. Time to first mission is the time from install to the first completed Twin mission, with a target of under ten minutes. Retention is the percentage of users who run a second mission within seven days, with a target of forty percent. The team reviews these indicators weekly, with a written note on any indicator that moves by more than five points.

### 13.4 Risks

Five risks dominate. **Prompt injection** is the risk that a malicious page or document misleads an agent into an unintended action. Mitigation is the layered defence in Section 9.2, with the capability matrix and Sentinel as the final barriers. **Model cost** is the risk that grounding and token costs exceed the subscription price. Mitigation is the budget envelope, caching, and monthly unit economics review. **Platform change** is the risk that Chrome, Gemini, or Workspace APIs change in a breaking way. Mitigation is version pinning, a compatibility test suite, and a fallback to the previous API version. **Regulatory change** is the risk that GDPR, EU AI Act, or CCPA obligations change. Mitigation is a compliance review on every major release and a data protection impact assessment. **Brand risk** is the risk that the product is perceived as a Google product. Mitigation is the brand review, the distinct name and identity, and the explicit disclaimer in every surface. Each risk has an owner, a mitigation, and a trigger that escalates it to the product lead.

### 13.5 Open questions

Three open questions remain. First, should custom agents be allowed to define their own spend limits, or should spend limits be set by the user only? The blueprint favours user-set limits, but the question requires user research. Second, should the Recorder's data be stored on-chain for immutability, or is Drive persistence sufficient? The blueprint favours Drive, with a hash of the record stored in the mission object for verification, but the question requires a security review. Third, should the system support multi-user approval chains, where two users must approve a payment? The blueprint defers this to the expansion phase, but the question requires enterprise customer input. Each open question is tracked in the repository, with a decision date and an owner.

### Appendix E. Glossary

Recorder is the flight log for a mission. Passport is the agent identity card. Sentinel is the safety agent. Bee is the payment agent. Scribe is the persistence agent. Nova is the orchestrator. Scout is the search agent. Octo is the ranking agent. Space is a context scope for tabs, apps, and missions. Gate is a human approval point. Envelope is a mission budget.
